Most of it ends up in landfills
The majority of discarded textiles go to landfill. Synthetic fibers — polyester, nylon — are plastic. They take 200 to 500 years to break down, leaching microplastics and chemical dyes into soil and groundwater throughout. Natural fibers decompose anaerobically and release methane, a greenhouse gas roughly 80 times more potent than CO₂ over 20 years. Once in landfill, almost nothing comes back out.
A lot of it is burned
Incinerating unsold inventory is common practice — used primarily as a brand-protection measure. It releases CO₂, nitrous oxide, and toxic compounds from synthetic dyes. It destroys all material value. $500 billion worth of textiles go this way every year. No second use, no recovery.
Thrift and donation absorb a fraction
Much donated clothing cannot be resold — low quality, wrong sizes. It gets bundled and shipped to developing economies, displacing local textile markets, or ends up in landfills there instead. Downcycling — shredding into insulation or rags — is a one-way process. Less than 1% of textiles are recycled back into new clothing fiber.
70% of returns are caused by fit, not preference
Of the $218 billion in fashion returns annually, 70% come from fit and sizing problems. 18% of returned goods are written off entirely — too costly to inspect or repackage. This is entirely avoidable waste, caused by the structural mismatch between standardized production and the people it's supposed to serve.
What's being done
Extended Producer Responsibility regulations, fiber-to-fiber recycling pilots, resale platforms, rental models — all real, all growing. But they work downstream of overproduction. They manage waste after it exists. None address the production model that keeps generating it. As long as the industry makes more than it sells, in sizes that don't fit most people, the problem compounds.
The only durable fix is producing only what is actually demanded. We are building the infrastructure to do that.

Most of the world's produced textiles end up in landfills without ever being used.

We believe this problem doesn't just need another waste management program. It needs to be solved from the core.

Why was textile waste so much lower two or three decades ago? Because garments were produced only when actually demanded — made to order. No overstock, no fit-driven returns. But that wasn't scalable, so the industry moved to mass production and bulk inventory.

We believe scalability is a solvable problem.

We are building machines that enable production of on-demand garments as efficiently as bulk production.
When landfills stop receiving waste from overproduction and returns — when every garment is produced only because someone asked for it — we will know we have succeeded.

What we're building.

Software
A dimensionally accurate 3D digital clone of you
Built from a quick body scan, the clone lets you create or choose designs and see how each garment fits on your actual body before anything is made. Every measurement precise. Every design previewed before a stitch is cut.
Marketplace
A network connecting you with on-demand makers
Boutiques and tailors who already know how to make one garment at a time, with skill. They receive your order with a precision-generated pattern, make it, and deliver it. The infrastructure for custom manufacturing already exists — we make it reachable.
Machinery
Robotics and automation built for on-demand production
Automation setups that will eventually let us fulfill each garment as efficiently as standardized bulk production does today. This is where on-demand stops being a premium and becomes the default.
